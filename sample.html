From A Genuine Fellowship: The Cross Radius

Chapter One

A Well Named Nopech

Read it the way the book asks to be read: a chapter at a sitting, unhurried. This is the first of thirty-two.

There is a road from my childhood that I can still walk with my eyes closed: eighteen kilometers of highland track between our home and my aunt’s village, red dust and stubborn stones, ridges that promised the village was near and then unfolded into one more valley. We walked it because legs were what we had, and because eighteen kilometers is not a distance to a child so much as a whole country: a country with its own birds, its own hunger, and its own particular thirst.

At my aunt’s village there was a well. And here I must slow down, because the well is why I have brought you on this road at all.

The well had a name, and I owe you the truth of where it came from before I use it. I never learned what the men who dug that well, generations before me, had called it, if they called it anything at all. But I knew a water in my aunt’s village growing up that my whole family spoke of the way you speak the name of an elder, and I have borrowed that name for the well of this chapter, because reverence, once you have owned it, deserves to be spent again. I gave this well its name myself: Nopech, spoken the way you spoke the name of an elder, with a certain weight, the way you said the name of a grey-haired man whose word could settle a quarrel. Nobody explained that weight to me as a boy and nobody needed to; children absorb reverence long before they can spell it. As a small boy I half believed that if you greeted Nopech politely enough, one of these years it might answer.

I can still stand at its rim in my memory. The stones of the mouth were polished smooth by generations of hands, the way door posts are polished by the shoulders of a family. Out of the dark shaft came a breath of cool air, and with it the smell of deep water and wet earth, a smell older than anyone in the village. If you leaned over, far below you could see a small coin of light trembling on darkness: the sky, looking back up at you from under the ground. Drop a pebble and Nopech answered, after a pause, with a soft, round syllable of water.

So the water was there. Hear me: the water was really there. You could smell it, you could see the sky floating on it, you could make it speak. Whoever had dug that well long before my time had done honest work; the shaft was true, the walls held, the depth was real. Nothing below the ground had failed.

But in those days, on the days I remember, Nopech had no rope and no bucket.

And I stood at its rim, a boy with the dust of eighteen kilometers in his throat, looking down at more water than I could drink in a lifetime, and I could not touch one drop of it.

Thirsty, at a well.

I did not know, at that age, that I was standing inside a parable, and that I would spend the rest of my life meeting people who live at that rim: people standing directly over everything they need, perishing politely for lack of a way to draw. This book begins at Nopech because your life, whether you have named it yet or not, has a Nopech in it. And because the first thing I have to tell you is the thing nobody told the boy at the rim: your thirst is not a malfunction.

It is a map.

Everyone Is Carrying a Jar

Come with me now to another well, older than Nopech and more famous, in a Samaritan town called Sychar: “Now Jacob’s well was there” (John 4:6). Scripture is careful to tell us the hour too: “and it was about the sixth hour” (John 4:6). Noon. John tells us the hour; he does not tell us why she chose it. But drawing water was morning work and evening work, done in company, with talk and laughter, for the well was the news outlet of the ancient world, and generations of readers have looked at a woman coming alone in the dead center of the heat and seen someone managing not her water but her shame: a woman who had arranged her day around the faces she could not bear to meet, because her story had been told in that town more often than she had told it: five marriages behind her, and the man at home now not her husband. I read her noon with those readers, as a kept schedule of avoidance, and her story bears the reading.

And on her head or her hip she carried the tool of her survival: a jar.

Look at that jar for a moment, because you own one. The jar was her system: walk, draw, carry, drink, thirst again, return tomorrow, and the day after, and the day after that. A jar is what thirst looks like once it has learned to manage itself. It does not heal anything; it administrates. It turns an ache into an errand.

We are a race of jar-carriers. One person’s jar is a career, refilled with every small promotion and empty again by the drive home. Another’s is romance: surely the next face, the next beginning, will be water at last. There is the approval jar, which leaks faster than any vessel I know; there is a glowing jar most of us now carry in a pocket and draw from every few minutes, even in the middle of prayers. And, I say it gently, there is a religious jar: services attended, duties done, a reputation for godliness carefully topped up, while the soul underneath is as dusty as that highland road. Jesus said one sentence over every jar ever carried: “Whosoever drinketh of this water shall thirst again” (John 4:13). Not because the water is poison. Because the water is water, and you were built for something water cannot do. The jar never argues; it simply requires refilling a little sooner each year, and we oblige.

Now watch who is waiting at the well at noon. Jesus, “being wearied with his journey, sat thus on the well” (John 4:6): tired, dusty, human, in the full heat, at the exact hour of the woman with the arranged schedule. And His opening word to her is not a sermon and not a rebuke. It is a request: Give me to drink. He begins by being thirsty in front of her. He asks something of her before He offers anything to her, which is a courtesy so deep most of us have never received it from another human being: He treats the town scandal as someone who has something to give.

She deflects, of course. She raises the ethnic wall, for the Jews have no dealings with the Samaritans; a little later she will raise the theological wall, the old dispute about which mountain hosts the true worship. She talks about the geography of worship to avoid talking about the thirst in her chest, and she would not be the last person to hide from God inside a religious discussion. But in between the walls she says the sentence that could be carved on the rim of Nopech and on the rim of the modern world:

“Sir, thou hast nothing to draw with, and the well is deep” (John 4:11).

She said it about Him. I have heard the whole human race say it about God. The well is deep: granted, there may be something down there, some depth under reality, some rumor of water. But there is no rope in sight, and I have a jar that works, more or less, and the day is hot. So we adjust our schedules, and we keep drawing.

Thirst Is Older Than You

But where does a thirst like hers come from? Do not say five marriages; the marriages were not the thirst, they were five trips to the well. The thirst was there before the first of them, or there would have been no first of them. And your thirst was there before whatever you have been blaming it on. To find its source you must read the map backward, much further back than your own story.

At the beginning, Scripture shows us a tender detail of geography: “And a river went out of Eden to water the garden” (Genesis 2:10). We were created downstream. Man was placed where the water of God already flowed, made to live in a garden where God Himself walked, fellowship running past his door like a river he never dug and never earned. That is the original human address. And when that fellowship broke, we did not lose the need of it; we lost only the supply. The need remained in us like a riverbed after the river: dry, shaped exactly like water, aching in the shape of what once filled it.

That is what your thirst is. It is not a defect in your design; it is your design, bereaved. It is the homesickness of a race born far from its first address, a memory in the bones of a garden your eyes have never seen and your heart has never stopped missing.

The psalmist knew the sensation and gave it its truest voice: “As the hart panteth after the water brooks, so panteth my soul after thee, O God. My soul thirsteth for God, for the living God” (Psalm 42:1–2). Consider that deer for a moment. It does not pant because it is defective. It pants because it is a deer, because running has spent it, and because there is such a thing in the world as a brook. Its thirst corresponds to something. And here I will set down the sentence I most want you to carry out of this chapter, the one aphorism I have repeated to more thirsty people than I can count:

Hunger is not evidence that food is imaginary.

Your thirst, honestly faced, is the most theological thing you have ever felt. At three in the morning, when the accomplishments have gone quiet and the jar stands empty by the bed, your thirst out-argues every clever doubt you own: it insists, against all fashionable despair, that you were made for Someone, the way panting insists on water. The ache is an arrow. Follow it backward and it lands in a garden; follow it forward, and I will tell you shortly where it lands.

But there is a tragedy to name first, and God Himself named it with a grief you can still hear in the sentence: “For my people have committed two evils; they have forsaken me the fountain of living waters, and hewed them out cisterns, broken cisterns, that can hold no water” (Jeremiah 2:13). The accusation fell first on covenant Israel; it has reached every heart that repeats the exchange. Two evils, not one. The first is leaving the fountain. The second is what we do next, and notice the word hewed: cisterns are hard work. We sweat over them. A fountain gives from itself, endlessly, from below; a cistern only holds what you can carry to it, and then only if it holds at all. And a cistern does not fail where you can watch it. It fails from within, below the waterline, in a place no passerby was watching, and you learn the truth on the dry day you finally need everything it was supposed to keep.

That can hold no water. Read the ledger slowly.

That is the honest accounting of every jar I have ever carried, and I have carried several: labored for, defended, decorated, empty. The tragedy of the human race is not that we are thirsty. It is where we have been digging. And when thirsty people finally despair of their diggings but still cannot face the fountain, they do a stranger thing yet: they hide. There are footsteps coming in the next chapter, and trees enough for all of us; I will meet you there. For now, stay at the rim.

The Water Comes to the Rim

Come back to Sychar with me, and this time look only at the One sitting on the well.

John has already told us who He is, in the widest sentence ever written: “All things were made by him; and without him was not any thing made that was made” (John 1:3). All things. Water, then. Every aquifer under Samaria, every river out of Eden, every cloud, every brook the hart ever panted after, and the throat of the woman walking toward Him with her jar. The Maker of oceans sat on the rim of a well He had made, wearied, and said to one avoided woman, Give me to drink.

Sit with that until it astonishes you. The wonder of this story is not that a sinful woman met God. It is where she met Him: not on the far mountain of her theological argument, not at the end of a long climb of reformation, but at the rim, at noon, at the exact coordinates of her daily thirst. She did not ascend to the Water. The Water came to the rim.

This is the gospel’s geometry, and it is unlike anything the race has hewed for itself. Nopech’s failure, remember, was never the water; the water was true. The failure was access: nothing to draw with, and the well is deep. Every religion I know of answers that problem the same way: it hands the thirsty a longer rope of effort and says, draw harder. Heaven’s answer was different in kind. Instruction had come down before, holy and good, and instruction could tell the thirsty where the water stood; it could not put one cupful in their hands. So this time heaven did not lower a rule. Heaven came down and sat on the rim, on the thirsty side of the problem, and spoke: “If thou knewest the gift of God, and who it is that saith to thee, Give me to drink; thou wouldest have asked of him, and he would have given thee living water” (John 4:10). Living water: in her ears that meant spring water, moving water, water alive from below, as against the flat, hauled, stored water of cisterns and jars. He was not offering her a better jar. Listen to the offer in full: “whosoever drinketh of the water that I shall give him shall never thirst; but the water that I shall give him shall be in him a well of water springing up into everlasting life” (John 4:14). In him. A spring on the inside: the end of the whole hauling economy, the riverbed finding its river again.

She reaches for it with both hands and, at first, entirely misunderstands it: “Sir, give me this water, that I thirst not, neither come hither to draw” (John 4:15). She is still thinking in jars; she wants a device that will spare her the noon errand. He does not scold the smallness of the request. He simply goes deeper, down past the errand to the ache, down into the five marriages and the sixth arrangement, telling her all things that ever she did, and, mark this, the exposure does not scorch her. Being fully known by Him became, that same afternoon, the best news she had ever carried; how that can be is a treasure I am keeping for a later chapter, and I will not spend it here. And He did not leave her in the comfort of being known. He lifted her past both mountains to the highest subject a soul can be handed, the Father seeking true worshippers, “in spirit and in truth” (John 4:23), for grace exposes in order to turn a person toward the truth.

But you have a question, and it is the right one. What about the rope? A well may be true and the Water may be willing, but how does deep water actually reach a person who has nothing to draw with? I am not going to answer that in this chapter; the answer is the costliest thing in this book, and it deserves its own ground. I will say only this much, and then hold my peace: when heaven finally solved the rope problem of the human race, it did not send down equipment.

Hold that.

And notice what John records next, one of those small details that carries a whole theology on its back: “The woman then left her waterpot” (John 4:28).

She left her waterpot.

John tells us only that she left it. I have never been able to read the leaving as less than a resignation: the management system lost its job that afternoon. The jar that had organized her shame, her schedule, and her survival stood abandoned against the stones, because you do not carry a cistern home from a fountain, and she went into the town she had been avoiding at noon and emptied it toward the well. Keep your eye on that abandoned jar; you will meet it again before this book ends.

Hold That Question

Now the road turns to you, as every road in this book will.

You have been thirsty in ways you do not publish. You have served and felt unheld; you have sung and stayed unsettled; you have reached the thing you were certain was water and stood there, cup in hand, wondering why the ache survived the achievement. Most of us do one of two things with that discovery. We are ashamed of it, because surely mature believers should not still be this thirsty, and so we hide the ache under busier service. Or we numb it, drawing faster and faster from jars we long ago stopped believing in. I am asking you, for the length of this book, to do a third thing, the thing the boy at Nopech could not help doing: stand at the rim and feel it honestly.

Your thirst is not your enemy. It is your escort. I do not mean that every craving tells the truth; desire since Eden is a compass that trembles, and some of its needles point at poison, as this book will say plainly when we reach the wells with other names. I mean the thirst beneath the cravings, the one that has outlived every jar you ever filled. That thirst is the truest map you own, and it was drawn before you were born: backward it runs to a garden and a river you were made beside; forward it runs, not to a technique, not to a program, not even to a doctrine, but to a Person, who is not ashamed to sit on wells at noon. The answer to your thirst has a Face, and in the second part of this book we will stop circling and look at that Face steadily, until looking becomes the beginning of drinking.

But not yet. This chapter refuses to resolve, and the refusal is deliberate: some questions must be carried until they have finished their work in us, and this is the first of them. So stand where the boy stood, over deep water, with the dust of your own long road in your throat, and let the question rise all the way up:

Why am I this thirsty?

Hold that question. Stay at the rim. Thirsty people are exactly what wells are for.

S E L A H

What is your jar: the thing you actually reach for first when the ache rises? Name it honestly. Not the answer you would give in church. The real one. And answer this beside it: how long does what it gives you last? And if no jar comes to mind, do not force one; tell Him that instead.

When did you last let yourself feel the thirst itself, without reaching for anything at all: no screen, no errand, no noise? Sit in it for ten unhurried minutes this week, with Psalm 42 open beside you, so the ache has somewhere true to look. What are you afraid it would say to you if you listened that long?

Jesus asked something of the woman before He offered anything to her; it was His courtesy at the rim. If that courtesy reached you this afternoon, Give me to drink, what do you have that He would ask for? Write it down, in plain words, and do not soften it.

P R A Y E R

Fountain of living waters, I come to You as a thirsty man comes, and for once I will not pretend otherwise. I confess the two evils as my own: I have forsaken You, and I have hewed my cisterns, and I have sweated over them, and I have decorated my jar, and I have blamed the road and the weather and the people when the water failed, and it has always failed. Thank You that my thirst was never Your absence but Your map, drawn in me before I could read. Thank You that You did not wait on the far side of the deep well for me to find a rope, but came wearied to the rim of my noon, and asked, and offered. Lord, I am the woman with the arranged schedule; I am the boy above water he cannot reach. Give me this water. Teach me, page by page through this book, to leave the waterpot where it stands. And until I understand the rope, keep me at the rim, thirsty and honest, looking for Your face: for You alone are the fountain of living waters, and my soul thirsteth for God, for the living God. Amen.

Here the sample rests.
