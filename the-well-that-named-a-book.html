The Well That Named a Book

By Kibrom Gero Gebre · August 2026

Every book has a door it entered through. Mine was a well.

There is a road from my childhood that I can still walk with my eyes closed: eighteen kilometers of highland track between our home and my aunt’s village, red dust and stubborn stones, ridges that promised the village was near and then unfolded into one more valley. We walked it because legs were what we had. At the end of that road, in my aunt’s village, there was a well.

When I came to write this book, I gave that well a name: Nopech. I owed my readers the truth of where the name came from, so I told them plainly in Chapter One: I never learned what the men who dug the well had called it. But I knew a water in that village growing up that my whole family spoke of the way you speak the name of an elder, and I borrowed that reverence, because reverence, once you have owned it, deserves to be spent again. Nobody explained that weight to me as a boy and nobody needed to; children absorb reverence long before they can spell it.

I can still stand at the well’s rim in my memory. The stones of the mouth were polished smooth by generations of hands. Out of the dark shaft came a breath of cool air, and with it the smell of deep water and wet earth. Drop a pebble, and the well answered, after a pause, with a soft, round syllable of water. The water was there. Hear me: the water was really there.

But on the days I remember, that well had no rope and no bucket. And I stood at its rim, a boy with the dust of eighteen kilometers in his throat, looking down at more water than I could drink in a lifetime, and I could not touch one drop of it. Thirsty, at a well.

I did not know, at that age, that I was standing inside a parable. I know it now, because I have spent years meeting people who live at that rim: people standing directly over everything they need, perishing politely for lack of a way to draw. Church people, many of them. Serving people. Singing people. Thirsty people.

That is why the book begins at Nopech and not at a pulpit. Because your life, whether you have named it yet or not, has a Nopech in it, and because the first thing I had to say could not wait past page one: your thirst is not a malfunction. It is a map. Where the map leads, and what heaven did about the rope, is the rest of the book; I will not spend it here.
